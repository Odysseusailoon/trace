MOONSHOT · INTERPRETABILITY

Edit the Weights in Natural Language, and Kill Post-Training

YUXI ZHENG · SEP 2026 · RESEARCH PITCH · NOT A RESULT
GOOGLE SCHOLAR· X · @VOIDASUKA· GITHUB

Changing a model's behaviour today costs a full forward pass, backward pass, RL step and eval cycle — even when the behaviour touches a fraction of a percent of the weights. Models get tamed like alchemy, not built like a computer. The bet: pretrain a sparse MoE with enough experts that behaviours land in named, addressable slots, then edit the slot instead of running the loop. If it works, post-training gets dramatically more precise and sample-efficient, and interpretability finally ships inside the production frontier models — the only place existential risk actually lives.

1 · The waste, stated plainly

A model that prefers pip over uv because its pretraining data was older does not need a new mental model of package management. It needs one routing decision redirected. Changing that means the whole loop, to move something affecting a sliver of the weights. You are being roughly four orders of magnitude more wasteful than the size of the actual change warrants.

The reason is that the model has no internal vocabulary for what it just learned. There is no slot labelled “package manager preference” for an engineer to edit. Gradient descent diffuses the change across millions of parameters, and the only way to undo it is to run the loop again.

Why post-hoc interpretability cannot close this

Sparse, interpretable structure does emerge inside trained models. But extracting it with an SAE means training an auxiliary model that is by definition smaller than the substrate it reads. That capacity gap is baked into the post-hoc approach; it is not a solvable engineering problem within it. And the fidelity loss compounds as the size gap grows, which is exactly the wrong direction for frontier scale.

So the only model that can interpret a frontier model is that model itself, which means interpretability has to be a training objective rather than an afterthought. It also has to ride on compute people are already willing to spend, because that spending happens whether or not interpretability comes along.

2 · The substrate is already being built

Every frontier lab now ships a sparse MoE, for capability and cost reasons that have nothing to do with interpretability. Expert count per layer, against ship date. Amber = Monet.

Monet (arXiv:2412.04139, Park, Ahn, Kim, Kang) folds sparse dictionary learning into end-to-end MoE pretraining rather than reconstructing it afterward, and pushes to 262,144 experts per layer — roughly 300× beyond the densest shipped model on this chart. It reports knowledge manipulation across domains, languages and toxicity without degrading general performance. That is the closest existing thing to named, addressable circuits at scale.

3 · Square-root scaling is what makes it affordable

Monet's expert decomposition scales total parameters with the square root of expert count. Without it, 262,144 experts is not an architecture, it is a bill.

This is the load-bearing engineering claim. A linear law makes monosemanticity a luxury nobody deploys; a square-root law makes it a design choice. It is also the claim most worth attacking, since it is the one that has to survive another two orders of magnitude of scale.

4 · Why agentic coding is the sharpest wedge

Coding fits this architecture for a structural reason: functions are tokens. If a model picks the right tokens in the right sequence it has produced the right function; there is no extra representation to bridge. That is plausibly why LLMs are already unusually good at code — the thing predicted and the thing that counts as success are the same object.

The actual bottleneck in long-horizon agentic coding is not generation, it is attribution. A model can diverge at any one of five hundred steps, and today there is no way to say which. It is teaching a child advanced mathematics by handing back only the final answer. Over ten steps you can find it by inspection; over five hundred you cannot, which is why current RL throws whole trajectories at a verifier and hopes the gradient sorts it out.

This part is not speculative — it is measured

The rest of this site is an attribution instrument built on exactly this premise. On a text-to-SQL task, twenty-one mirror replays rewind episodes to the same pre-decision state, where the outcome distribution is identical at o₀ = 0.22, and then replay after each episode's own first query.

One action, and the distribution goes to 0.00 or 1.00. That is attribution at step granularity, already working with an external verifier. The architectural bet is what happens when the model can do this internally, at every branch point, instead of once at the end: post-training coding goes from “generate five hundred steps, verify once, hope the signal is dense enough” to continuous verification against the model's own attribution.

This also solves the go-to-market problem that the safety framing alone does not. Every lab already wants better agentic coding, so the pitch does not require anyone to first buy an interpretability thesis. You do not win by being twenty percent better; you need one dimension where you are an order of magnitude better, and coding throughput is the dimension every lab already has a reason to care about.

5 · Why it must be model-driven, not human-labelled

The strongest form of the safety argument is not that post-hoc interpretability is slow. It is that known risks are not the risks that matter. If a problem is already known, the field already knows how to attach a post-training patch or a system-prompt guardrail, and that is a workaround dressed up as a solution. A model that “cannot do biology” because safety training made it refuse anything biology-adjacent has not been made safe against bioweapon risk — a system prompt saying “don't do biology” gets the identical outcome and needs no research programme to discover.

The risks that matter are the ones nobody thought to test for. A module built by a human, for a capability a human already named, only ever covers what humans anticipated. Give the model room to discover its own internal boundaries — the way an SAE sweeps for features without anyone specifying which concepts to look for — and you get coverage of risks nobody would have named. The cost is that unsupervised discovery still needs a way to label and act on what it finds, which is precisely the addressability problem this architecture exists to solve.

6 · The reliability threshold

Framed as nines. The value is not linear in accuracy; there is a phase change.

ablation accuracywhat it unlocks
~50%Curiosity. Not useful for anything downstream.
90%A viable first pass before reaching for a full RL cycle. A cheap filter that sometimes works.
99.99%The character of the problem changes. The loop goes on autopilot: the model runs its own post-training, automating identify-and-edit instead of requiring a human to curate data, run rollouts and evaluate.

There is probably still a human bottleneck for genuine preference calls. But most of the mechanical cost — a thousand rollouts, manual labelling, forward and backward passes for a change touching a sliver of the weights — disappears.

7 · What the architecture has to deliver

  • Behaviours of interest correspond to identifiable, named structures. The granularity need not be perfect, but a meaningful share of behaviours should be addressable rather than diffuse.
  • Edits to those structures produce predictable behaviour changes without retraining the rest.
  • It stays competitive at scale on standard pretraining metrics. Interpretability that costs twenty points of perplexity is interpretability that never gets deployed.
  • The interpretability is not an auxiliary system living below the model's own capacity. Same forward pass, same compute.

This is closer to the von Neumann picture than to the black-box transformer picture. Programs live in addressable memory, the execution loop is shared, and editing a program does not require rebuilding the CPU. Put more bluntly: if a language model plus persistent memory is already a new kind of Turing machine — more than one person has noticed that independently — the goal is to make that machine reliable rather than merely possible in principle. Chain-of-thought is already argued to be Turing-complete in the loose sense. It is nowhere near reliable. Reliability, not existence, is the open problem.

8 · The combination under test

Monet supplies the substrate: monosemantic experts at scale via sparse dictionary learning during pretraining. Percepta supplies a different proof of concept — its construction is analytical rather than learned, but the existence proof matters: deterministic operations, including a full WASM interpreter, can live inside a transformer's forward pass with exact correctness and competitive speed.

  • Pretrain a Monet-style model on standard data and objectives, exposing a subset of expert slots as compiler-grounded candidates.
  • On those slots, add an RL signal tied to compiler-verifiable micro-environments (arithmetic, regex, AST transforms, small WASM programs), each producing deterministic ground truth, so rewards are exact rather than learned.
  • The RL pressure pushes verifiable behaviours to route through those slots and to internally implement the verified operation, while routing itself stays learned at the boundary.
  • The rest of the network keeps its standard pretraining loss, so the grounded slots compose with the model instead of replacing it.
One disambiguation, up front

This is not “formal methods everywhere”. Verification is a training signal on a subset of expert slots, not a runtime proof over the whole model. The rest of the network trains and behaves like an ordinary pretrained model. Only the compiler-grounded slots carry the guarantee.

A load-bearing decision, sharpened over the summer: do not assume a full pretraining run is needed before testing anything. Current frontier open-weight MoEs may already carry enough sparsity to test the core premise — that expert count correlates with monosemanticity — without spending a dollar on pretraining. If the correlation holds and existing models are close enough, the product becomes tooling for editing existing weights rather than a from-scratch architecture bet.

9 · What I have not figured out

The honest section.

  • The training objective. What loss pushes compiler-verifiable behaviour into specific slots rather than diffusing it? Monet's monosemanticity loss is the anchor, but stacking a grounded RL term on top risks collapsing the dictionary structure.
  • Whether monosemanticity is even the right proxy. An expert can correlate with a concept without being causally responsible for the behaviour. Measuring causal responsibility separately is unsolved. One alternative kept in the back pocket, not pursued: give experts shared, competitive space to read and write, so boundaries are enforced by competition rather than hoped for from raw expert count. That is someone else's idea, floated in conversation, and the plan deliberately does not depend on it.
  • Routing stability under RL pressure. Monet's assignments are stable under pretraining loss. Not obvious they stay stable when a subset of slots is optimised differently. Worst case is monosemanticity decay across the broader pool.
  • Curriculum design. Arithmetic and regex are the obvious start; the right mix that gives signal without overfitting is unclear. Percepta's WASM substrate is suggestive but expensive.
  • Mode collapse on the grounded slots. Too sharp an RL pressure and the slots learn a trivial implementation that satisfies the verifier without generalising. The verifier becomes the adversary.
  • Capacity loss elsewhere. Even if the grounded slots work, the rest could degrade subtly. Monet's ablations are encouraging, not conclusive.
  • The minimum viable first experiment. Is the cheapest test a small Monet-style model with grounded slots on an arithmetic curriculum, or is it faster and more diagnostic to first exhaust what can be learned from ablating existing open-weight MoEs before training anything?
