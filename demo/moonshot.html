MOONSHOT PROJECT FOR INTERPRETABILITY

I want to build a model where you can edit the weights directly in natural language, and kill post-training entirely.

Right now, changing a model’s behaviour costs a full forward pass, backward pass, RL step, and eval cycle, even for something touching a fraction of a percent of the weights. I’ve worked on post-training data, and the amount of money and compute that gets burned moving one narrow behaviour is absurd. Models get “tamed” through training like alchemy, not built like a computer, and that’s both wasteful and basically impossible to interpret.

The reason this has to happen at pretraining, not bolted on after: a smaller model used to interpret a bigger one has to compress it, which is literally what SAEs do, and a human can’t know in advance every risk a much smarter model might contain. The only thing that can actually interpret a frontier model is the frontier model itself, and building that in after the fact is too expensive. It has to be part of the pretraining run.

The industry is already moving toward sparser mixture-of-experts models anyway, every frontier lab ships one now, and Monet already shows a version of this works: pretrain a sparse MoE with enough experts and you get monosemantic, addressable circuits instead of a polysemantic mess, at close to no cost in performance. There’s no obvious reason this stops working with more scale and the right tweaks, which is exactly what the current experiments are testing.

If it works, this cuts both ways on purpose. Getting monosemanticity close to perfect at close to no performance cost makes post-training dramatically more precise, and more compute- and sample-efficient. It also means interpretability actually makes it into the production frontier models people train and deploy at scale, which is the only place existential risk actually lives and where the compute and money already are.

The problem in more detail

Frontier model interpretability has to use a model as large as the target model, for a few reasons that all point the same direction:

  • At the level of existential risk, we need to interpret frontier models at scale. Interpretability methods have to work on the largest models, not toy versions of them.
  • We should use methods that scale with compute, so pouring in more resources makes the method better instead of exposing its limits.
  • Using a smaller model, especially of a different architecture, to interpret a larger model forces semantic compression, and the fidelity loss compounds as the size gap grows.
  • Given the above, the only model that can interpret the largest frontier models is the model itself. That means interpretability has to be baked into training as an objective or loss, not added afterward.
  • We should use methods people are already willing to spend enormous compute on for production models, since that spending happens regardless of whether interpretability comes along for the ride.

Post-training is the dominant lever for behaviour control now, and the loop is expensive even when the target behaviour is trivial. A model that prefers pip over uv because its pretraining data was older doesn’t need a new mental model of package management, it needs one routing decision redirected. Changing that means a full forward pass, backward pass, RL step, and eval cycle, just to move something that affects a fraction of a percent of the weights. You’re being ten thousand times more wasteful than the size of the actual change warrants.

The model has no internal vocabulary for what it just learned. There’s no slot labelled “package manager preference” that an engineer can edit directly. The change gets diffused across millions of parameters by gradient descent, and the only way to undo or modify it is to run the loop again. Sparse, interpretable structure does emerge inside trained models, but extracting it with post-hoc methods like sparse autoencoders means training an auxiliary model that’s by definition smaller than the substrate it’s trying to read. That capacity gap is baked into the post-hoc approach. It’s not a solvable engineering problem within it.

Every frontier lab is already moving toward sparser mixture-of-experts architectures for capability and cost reasons that have nothing to do with interpretability, which means the substrate this bet needs is getting built anyway. Monet already shows a working version at moderate scale: push expert count up during pretraining and you get monosemantic, addressable circuits instead of a superposed mess, without an obvious tax on performance. There’s no clear reason that breaks down with more scale or the right routing tweaks. Confirming that, not guessing at it, is what the current ablation sweep is for.

Why it must be model-driven, not human-labelled

The strongest form of the safety argument isn’t that post-hoc interpretability is slow. It’s that known risks aren’t the risks that matter. If a problem is already known, the field already knows how to attach a post-training patch or a system-prompt guardrail to it, and that’s not a real solution, just a workaround dressed up as one. A model that “can’t do biology” because its safety training made it refuse anything biology-adjacent hasn’t been made safe against bioweapon risk. A system prompt saying “don’t do biology” would get you the identical outcome, and wouldn’t need a research programme to discover. The risks that actually matter are the ones nobody thought to test for, because no human declared “focus on this domain” in advance.

This is also why unsupervised discovery beats human-curated modules: a module built by a human, for a capability a human already named, only ever covers what humans anticipated. If the model has more room to discover its own internal boundaries, even crudely, the way sparse autoencoders sweep for features without a human specifying which concepts to look for, you get coverage of risks nobody would have thought to name. The tradeoff is that unsupervised discovery still needs a way to label and act on what it finds, which is exactly the addressability problem this whole architecture is trying to solve.

The reliability threshold, and why it matters practically

Framed as a nines-of-reliability problem, the tiers look like this:

~50%At roughly 50% ablation accuracy, the method isn’t useful for anything beyond curiosity.
90%At 90%, it becomes a viable first pass to try before reaching for a full RL cycle, a cheap filter that sometimes works.
99.99%At 99.99%, the character of the problem changes. You can put the loop on autopilot. The model runs its own post-training in a loop, automating the identify-and-edit cycle instead of requiring a human to curate data, run rollouts, and evaluate results.

There will likely still be a human bottleneck somewhere, if only for genuine preference calls, but most of the mechanical cost of post-training (a thousand rollouts, manual labelling, forward and backward passes for a change touching a sliver of the weights) disappears.

The architectural hope

What I want from a model architecture, in priority order:

  • Behaviours of interest correspond to identifiable, named structures inside the network. The granularity doesn’t have to be perfect, but a meaningful share of behaviours should be addressable rather than diffuse.
  • Edits to those structures produce predictable changes to behaviour without retraining the rest of the model.
  • The architecture stays competitive with non-interpretable architectures at scale on standard pretraining metrics. Interpretability that costs twenty points of perplexity is interpretability that never gets deployed.
  • The interpretability isn’t bolted on with an auxiliary system living below the model’s own capacity. It’s part of the same forward pass, trained on the same compute.

This is closer to the von Neumann picture of a computer than to the current black-box transformer picture. Programs live in addressable memory. The execution loop is shared. Editing a program doesn’t require rebuilding the CPU. A cruder but more visceral framing that has landed better in conversation: if a language model plus persistent memory is already, in some sense, a new kind of Turing machine (more than one person I’ve pitched this to has noticed that independently), the goal is to make that Turing machine reliable rather than merely possible in principle. Chain-of-thought reasoning inside a transformer is already argued to be Turing-complete in the loose sense. It’s just nowhere near reliable yet. Reliability, not existence, is the actual open problem.

The combination, concrete

Monet supplies the architectural substrate. Horizontal and vertical decomposition gets expert counts up to 262,144 per layer with square-root parameter scaling, while keeping experts monosemantic via sparse dictionary learning during pretraining. Existing demonstrations show clean knowledge manipulation across domains, languages, and toxicity mitigation without degrading general performance. This is the closest existing approach to named, addressable circuits at scale.

Percepta supplies the proof of concept for the kind of behaviours that can be compiled into transformer weights. Its core construction is analytical rather than learned, but the existence proof matters: deterministic operations, including a full WASM interpreter, can live inside a transformer’s forward pass with exact correctness and competitive inference speed.

The combination under test:

  • Pretrain a Monet-style model on standard data with standard objectives, exposing a subset of expert slots as compiler-grounded candidates.
  • On those candidate slots, add an RL signal tied to a curriculum of compiler-verifiable micro-environments (arithmetic, regex matching, AST transformations, small WASM programs), each producing deterministic ground truth so rewards are exact rather than learned.
  • The RL pressure on those slots pushes verifiable behaviours to route through them and to internally implement the verified operation, while routing itself stays learned at the boundary.
  • The rest of the network keeps its standard pretraining loss, so the compiler-grounded slots compose with the rest of the model instead of replacing it.
One disambiguation worth stating up front

Since it’s the fastest way a sharp technical reader dismisses this: this isn’t “formal methods everywhere.” Verification is a training signal applied to a subset of expert slots, not a runtime proof carried over the whole model. The rest of the network trains and behaves exactly like an ordinary pretrained model. Only the compiler-grounded slots carry the verified-behaviour guarantee.

A load-bearing decision, sharpened over the summer: don’t assume the full pretraining run is necessary before testing anything. Current frontier open-weight MoEs (Kimi K3 at roughly 800 experts, DeepSeek V4 at 384 routed plus one shared, GLM, MiniMax) may already carry enough sparsity to test the core premise, that scaling expert count correlates with monosemanticity, without spending a single dollar on a pretraining run. If the correlation holds and existing models are already close enough, the actual product becomes tooling for editing existing weights rather than a from-scratch architecture bet.

Why agentic coding is the sharpest wedge

Coding fits this architecture unusually well for a simple structural reason: functions are tokens. If a model picks the right tokens in the right sequence, it has produced the right function. There’s no extra layer of representation to bridge. This is plausibly why LLMs are already unusually good at coding relative to other domains: the thing being predicted and the thing that counts as success are the same object.

The actual bottleneck in long-horizon agentic coding isn’t code generation, it’s attribution. A model working through a five-hundred-step agentic coding trajectory can diverge from the correct path at any one of those steps, and right now there’s no way to pinpoint which step it was, the equivalent of teaching a child advanced mathematics by giving them only the final answer and no way to identify which line of their own working went wrong. Over ten steps this is tractable by inspection. Over five hundred, it’s practically impossible to localise, which is exactly why current RL post-training for coding throws entire trajectories at a verifier and hopes the gradient sorts it out.

If a model architecture could perfectly attribute which expert or path is responsible for which behaviour, that ablation could run automatically at every step of a rollout, not just at the end. That upgrades post-training coding from “generate five hundred steps, verify once, hope the signal is dense enough” to “verify continuously, at every branch point, using the model’s own internal attribution.” Pushed to the extreme: a sufficiently large, sufficiently sparse model could run verification against its own knowledge of code entirely inside its own weights, a compiler living inside the weights, verifying its own outputs without needing an external verifier loop for every rollout. That’s speculative and needs testing, but it’s the direct, literal endpoint of “functions are tokens plus perfect attribution.”

Rysana AI is the existing evidence this direction isn’t fantasy. They already got roughly a thousand-fold improvement in coding throughput over standard approaches in 2024, using some combination of mixture-of-experts and constrained decoding (the exact mechanism is proprietary and unconfirmed by outside verification, so treat this as suggestive rather than validated, not a load-bearing citation). The point isn’t to compete with them on throughput. It’s that if a comparable throughput gain is achievable through routing and token-level structure, a comparable gain from attribution-driven, verifier-light post-training is plausible too. It’s the four-minute mile: proof the barrier isn’t physical, even without knowing precisely how it was broken.

This wedge also solves the go-to-market problem the safety framing alone doesn’t. Every frontier lab already wants to improve agentic coding, so this isn’t a hard sell requiring anyone to first believe an interpretability thesis. A pitch that says “this measurably improves sample efficiency on long-horizon coding post-training” gets taken seriously by an audience that would otherwise not care about attribution or interpretability for their own sake. The Elon Musk framing applies directly here: you don’t win by being twenty percent better, you need one dimension where you’re an order of magnitude better, and coding throughput is the dimension every lab already has a reason to care about.

What I have not figured out yet

This is the honest section, updated to reflect where the open problems actually sit as of September.

  • Training objective formalism. What’s the precise loss that pushes compiler-verifiable behaviour into specific expert slots rather than diffusing it across the MoE? Monet’s existing monosemanticity loss is the natural anchor, but stacking a compiler-grounded RL term on top needs careful design to avoid collapsing the dictionary structure.
  • Whether monosemanticity is even the right proxy. An expert can correlate with a concept without being causally responsible for the behaviour associated with it. It’s not yet clear how to measure causal responsibility separately from raw monosemanticity, or whether it matters enough to be worth a dedicated fix versus something the current ablation sweep already surfaces as a side effect. One alternative worth keeping in the back pocket, not pursuing yet: instead of a bare per-token router, give experts some shared, competitive space to read from and write to, so cleaner boundaries get enforced by competition rather than hoped for from raw expert count alone. This is someone else’s idea, floated in conversation, not mine to take further without them, and the current plan deliberately doesn’t depend on it. If the brute-force expert-count approach stalls, it’s the first place to look.
  • Routing stability under RL pressure. Monet’s expert assignments are stable under standard pretraining loss. It’s not obvious they stay stable when a subset of slots gets optimised under a different objective. The worst failure mode is monosemanticity decay across the broader expert pool.
  • Curriculum design for compiler-grounded environments. Arithmetic and regex are the obvious starting points, but the right mix of micro-environments to give signal without overfitting is unclear. Percepta’s WASM substrate is suggestive but expensive.
  • Mode collapse on the compiler-grounded slots. If the RL pressure is too sharp, the slots might learn a trivial implementation that satisfies the verifier without generalising. The verifier becomes the adversary.
  • Capacity loss elsewhere in the network. Even if the compiler-grounded slots work, the rest of the model could degrade in subtle ways. Existing Monet ablations are encouraging but not conclusive on this point.
  • The minimum viable first experiment. Is the right cheapest test a small Monet-style model with compiler-grounded slots on an arithmetic curriculum, or is it faster and more diagnostic to first exhaust what can be learned from ablating existing frontier open-weight MoEs before training anything new?
